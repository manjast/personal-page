# Variant A+C — Hybrid: Operator's Notebook body + Typewriter Spec cover/cite

**Reference mood**: A vintage technical document with modern content. The cover establishes the document's identity (vintage typewriter spec); the body is the working substance (operator's notebook); the cite closes the document (vintage typewriter spec, matching the cover). The result: a coherent identity with a readable body.

**Why this hybrid?** The pure variants each have a tradeoff:
- **Pure A (Operator's Notebook)**: best voice match, best differentiation, but the cover slide is "just a notebook page" — pleasant but not distinctive.
- **Pure C (Typewriter Spec)**: best differentiation, but the all-mono body is uncomfortable for long stretches on a phone.

The hybrid splits the difference: the cover + cite get the vintage treatment (unusual + memorable + sets the document's identity); the body gets the notebook treatment (readable + voice-aligned). Two style-switches (cover→body, body→cite) keep the design coherent — not 6 style-switches.

**Palette** (uses BOTH variants' palettes, with a clear semantic split):
- **Body slides (2, 3, 4, 5)**: notebook palette
  - Paper: `#F5F2EC` (cream)
  - Ink: `#1A1A1A`
  - Accent: `#B85C3D` (terracotta)
  - Hand-drawn-feel SVG diagrams
  - Serif h1 + mono body
- **Cover + cite slides (1, 6)**: typewriter palette
  - Paper: `#F0EBD8` (aged paper)
  - Ink: `#2B2A28`
  - Accent: `#B83227` (red)
  - Mono everything + scanline overlay
  - "Stamp" header in the top-left + "page x / 6" stamp in the top-right

**Why this split is right**:
- The cover slide's job is to establish identity. Vintage typewriter spec is more distinctive than notebook page.
- The body slides' job is to carry substance. Notebook is more readable than typewriter for 4 slides of content.
- The cite slide's job is to close. Matching the cover creates a frame (vintage→content→vintage).

**Why 2 style-switches is the right number, not 6**:
- 6 style-switches (alternating every slide) would feel chaotic. The reader would never settle into the document.
- 2 style-switches (cover→body, body→cite) feel like a frame. The reader is "inside" the body for 4 slides.

**Voice match**: 9/10. The cover signals "vintage technical document" (signals precision + care). The body signals "operator's notebook" (signals operator, not influencer). The cite closes the frame.

**Differentiation**: 9/10. The hybrid is rarer than any pure variant. Most carousels pick one aesthetic; the hybrid is "this is two aesthetics working together."

**Algorithm fit**: 8/10. Dwell time high (the cover invites "what is this?" + the body carries substance). Save rate high (the cover is memorable; the body is reference-worthy). Comment rate moderate-high (the closing question is preserved).

**Practical fit**: 8/10. The hybrid is harder to design than a pure variant (the style-switch must be invisible — not jarring). But once the design is done, it's no harder to render or upload.

**Risk**: Medium. The hybrid might read as "two designs glued together" if the style-switch is too abrupt. Mitigation: the cover and cite share the same "stamp" + scanline overlay, so the visual frame is consistent. The body slides share the same hand-drawn-feel + terracotta accent, so the content is consistent.

**My recommendation**: this is the optimal choice. The hybrid captures the best of both worlds: the cover/cite signal "vintage precision" (the most distinctive aesthetic of the 5), the body delivers substance in a readable format.
