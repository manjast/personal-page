OpenAI switches off `gpt-4o-mini` on January 14, 2027. A lot of quiet production code runs on it: classifiers, extractors and routers built in 2024 that worked well enough and were never touched again.

The obvious migration is one line. Replace the model ID with the named successor, `gpt-6-luna`, and ship.

I ran exactly that on a support-message router. Same prompt, same confidence threshold of 0.95, new model ID. Before the change, no message went to the wrong team without a human check. After it, 3.9% did. That is 39 customers in every 1,000: the person whose card was just stolen, waiting in the queue for address changes. No error, no alert. Every response was valid JSON with a confident answer.

The new model is not worse at routing. The threshold is the problem. A 0.95 from `gpt-4o-mini` and a 0.95 from Luna are different promises, and nothing in your code knows that.

## Two numbers run a router

A customer message comes in. The model picks one of 50 team queues and says how sure it is. Above a threshold, the message is routed automatically. Below it, a person reads it.

- **Misroute rate**: the share of all messages sent automatically to the wrong queue. This is the risk.
- **Automation rate**: the share of all messages nobody has to read. This is the value.

Turn the threshold up and both drop. The whole game is: how much can you automate while misroutes stay under a limit you are willing to live with?

I tested four ways out of `gpt-4o-mini` on 9,758 Banking77 test messages:

- **Luna** (`gpt-6-luna`), OpenAI's named successor;
- **Claude Haiku 5.5**, the obvious rival;
- **Jev**, TypeSafe's decision model, which returns probabilities instead of text;
- **OpenAI's Decisions API**, the same Luna behind a decision-model interface.

Every model got its own threshold, fitted on separate development messages. The rules were written down and frozen in git before a single test message was sent.

## gpt-4o-mini has a cliff, not a dial

Look at the old model first. It is worse than most teams think.

`gpt-4o-mini` speaks in steps of 0.05, and it is confident about almost everything. On the development messages it said 0.90 for 76% of the answers it routed. 470 of those 1,994 answers were wrong. Only 50 answers got 0.95.

So the dial has two positions:

- **0.95**: 2% of messages automated. Safe and useless.
- **0.90**: 72% automated, and 15% of all messages misrouted. One in five of the messages it routes on its own goes to the wrong team.

Nothing in between. If your `gpt-4o-mini` classifier gates at 0.9, go and count what that gate lets through.

## The winner depends on how many mistakes you accept

A 2% misroute limit means 20 customers in 1,000 sent to the wrong desk. That is a business choice, not a law of nature. A fraud queue might tolerate 1%. A queue where a wrong route costs a 30-second handover might tolerate 10%.

So I ran the comparison at five limits. Each model gets its own threshold per limit. The table shows automation; higher is better.

**The migration is free capacity.** Between a 2% and a 10% limit, every LLM replacement automates 25-64% of messages. `gpt-4o-mini` automates 2%. On a router handling 10,000 messages a day, that is up to 6,000 messages a day that nobody has to read, at the same risk.

**There is no best model.** Haiku leads at 1%. Luna with an adapted prompt leads at 2%. Jev leads at 5% and 10%. Any leaderboard that names one winner for this job answers a question nobody asked.

**At 15%, the race is over.** If your business already lives with `gpt-4o-mini` at 0.90, a replacement buys you little beyond staying online.

So the order of decisions is fixed. First the misroute limit, agreed with whoever owns the queue. Then a threshold per model, on your own data. Only then the model.

## The token price is not your bill

TypeSafe sells Jev as "193.6x Faster, 444.6x Cheaper" than LLMs, with an input price "238x lower" than Claude Fable 5.1. At $0.042 per million input tokens, it is cheap per token.

But a router does not pay per token. It pays per message, and every message carries the same baggage: the descriptions of all 50 queues, a few thousand tokens, sent again with every customer message.

Luna and Haiku can keep that baggage in a prompt cache and read it back at a tenth of the price. Jev and the Decisions API cannot. Think of a single ticket against a season pass: the single ticket is cheaper on the price board, and the season pass wins as soon as you travel every day.

On a busy router, Jev costs 3.7 times as much as Luna. On a quiet one, about half. The crossover comes early: at about two messages an hour, cached Luna is already the cheaper one.

And here is what the price board hides. **Jev wins on cost exactly where cost does not matter.** At 50 messages a day, every option on this list costs under a dollar a month. At 10,000 a day, the cache is always warm: Luna costs about $12 a month, Jev $44. At that volume, the difference between automating 2% and 43% of messages is 4,000 messages a day that nobody has to read. That is the real bill, and no API price comes close.

## Same model, two doors

The Decisions API is the cleanest experiment here: same Luna, different door.

I timed both doors from Belgrade, one request at a time, interleaved with every other model. Behind the Decisions door, Luna spent 0.08 seconds on OpenAI's servers at the median. Behind the normal API, 0.96 seconds. Twelve times faster, more than OpenAI's own "up to 10x". End to end, an answer took 0.28 seconds instead of 1.26.

It also cost six times as much per message as cached Luna, and it automated less: 25% of messages at a 2% limit, against 35%. Its confidence is rounded to two decimals and piles up at the top, just like Jev's.

So the Decisions API sells speed, not routing. For a ticket queue, where nobody notices a quarter of a second against a second and a quarter, that is money spent on the wrong thing. For live chat or voice, it could be the whole point.

## Jev: right answers, blunt confidence

Jev is the most interesting result, because its answers are good. On in-scope development messages its top pick was right 85.6% of the time, against 84.2% for `gpt-4o-mini`.

It is also fast. From Belgrade, Jev answered in 0.28 seconds at the median, with the tightest tail of anything I tested: 95% of answers within 0.35 seconds. The small LLMs took 1.0 to 1.3 seconds. That is four times faster, a real advantage for anything a customer waits on. It is not the "193.6x Faster" on TypeSafe's homepage, whose own example puts an LLM at 8.6 seconds per task. Small models without reasoning answer a router in about one second.

Its confidence is blunt. Jev rounds its probabilities to two decimals, so answers pile up at exactly 1.00. On the test set, 43% of all messages landed at 1.00, and one in 19 of those was wrong. There is nothing above 1.00 to tighten the gate with. Under a 2% limit, Jev automates nothing at all. The new 1.14 preview rounds the same way.

I tried a rescue with Jev's second signal, a separate yes/no probability that the message belongs to the catalog. It landed at 2.03% misroutes. Close, and a fail. It is published next to everything else, because a method that only reports its wins is marketing.

That is the lesson I would carry to any classifier, LLM or not: **a calibrated confidence score can still be useless for a risk gate.** The gate lives at the top of the scale, where you stop asking a human. If 40% of answers share the top value, your gate has one setting, and it is the wrong one.

## How it was run

Results like these are only worth something if the rules could not bend to fit them.

- The metrics, the threshold rule and the acceptance gates were written in a specification before any test message was sent.
- The rules were frozen with a git tag. Every test run records the tag it ran under, and the runner refuses to run the same configuration twice.
- Every decision record is generated from the raw recorded responses. CI regenerates all of them on every change and checks them byte for byte.
- Every later analysis was declared as an amendment before its test numbers existed, including the one that failed.

The whole study cost about $14.50 in API calls.

## What I got wrong

The registered verdicts said nothing. All five came back SHIP, because the gate only required each replacement not to automate 3 points less than the incumbent's 2%, and nothing can. I saw it after the incumbent's development run. Changing the gate then would have been moving the goalposts, so it stayed, and the real analysis went in as declared amendments.

The pilot already had the evidence. Of its 100 messages, `gpt-4o-mini` gave 74 a confidence of 0.90 and exactly one a 0.95. Five minutes with that histogram would have saved the registered analysis. It is now the first thing I would check in any classifier migration.

If you own a `gpt-4o-mini` classifier, the work is short:

1. Agree on the misroute limit with the queue owner.
2. Refit the threshold for every candidate on your own labelled messages. Never carry the old one over.
3. Look at the top of the confidence histogram before you trust a gate.
4. Price the model per message at your real traffic, not per token.

## Methodology note

- Data: Banking77 (PolyAI), pinned commit; 50 intents as queues, the rest as "none of these"; 3,075 development and 9,758 test messages. Clean, single-turn English; real support traffic is messier.
- Models: `gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18`, `gpt-6-luna` (Responses API without reasoning, and Decisions API), `claude-haiku-5-5` (no thinking), `jev-1.13.0` (TypeSafe's stable release; 1.14 was in preview). Test runs on October 22, 2026.
- Rates are shares of all test messages. \* marks a one-sided 95% Wilson upper bound at or above the limit. The 2% analysis was declared before any replacement ran; the Jev rescue and the five-limit table after the first test results.
- Cost: mean token usage per message at list prices on October 22, 2026. Warm cache prices the shared prompt as a cache read, cold as uncached input. Traffic figures assume random arrivals and one cache per prompt.
- Speed: a separate study from Belgrade over home Wi-Fi, 200 rounds with all six configurations interleaved, one request at a time, on October 22, 2026; server time from OpenAI's `openai-processing-ms` header. Latency was reported, never gated: a ticket queue does not need sub-second answers.
- Vendor claims as read on typesafe.ai and OpenAI's Decisions API guide on October 22, 2026.
- Code, specification, run records and decision records: [model-swap-check on GitHub](https://github.com/manjast/model-swap-check).
